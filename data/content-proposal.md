# Content proposal: what the documentation should consist of

Date: 2026-10-09. This is the second layer on top of the restructure. The tree says where pages live; this
proposal says which pages should exist at all. Every one of the 323 current pages gets exactly one
action, and each area lists the pages we should end up with. Rewriting the prose is the step after this and
is deliberately out of scope here.

## Principles applied

1. **Block-first.** The main documentation describes a fresh WordPress install: block editor, block theme,
   Site Editor. Classic-era material lives in the Classic WordPress section as a maintained appendix.
2. **Users, not developers.** Developer and sysadmin material goes to developer.wordpress.org, which already
   absorbed 62 former HelpHub pages. Only a handful of pages still qualify.
3. **One concept, once.** The block reference repeats its toolbar and settings paragraphs on every page;
   three maintenance pages carry the same checklist; several pairs say the same thing twice. Shared material
   gets one home and everything else links to it.
4. **Tasks where users have a task, reference where they look things up.** Screens stay as reference pages
   because wp-admin help tabs link to them. Site Editor, publishing and maintenance gain task pages.
5. **Freshness.** Pages describing removed or renamed features, or untouched since before 2024, are
   rewritten, merged or deleted rather than kept out of habit.
6. **Nothing core links to may break.** WordPress core links to 75 HelpHub slugs from help tabs,
   theme readmes, the About screen and the fatal-error notice (`data/core-linked-slugs.json`). Those slugs
   keep serving a page, or redirect to the exact section that replaces them. Pages linked from core are
   never moved off-site.

## What changes most

- **Block reference: 135 pages become 58.** One Embeds page with a provider table replaces 36
  near-identical provider pages. Theme blocks that are 15-word stubs today are documented on family pages
  (Post blocks, Post meta blocks, Site identity blocks) or on their container's page (comment blocks on
  Comments, Submenu and links on Navigation, Post Template on Query Loop). Content blocks keep one page
  each, trimmed of the repeated toolbar and settings paragraphs.
- **Site Editor gains task pages.** Edit your header and footer, change colors and fonts with Styles,
  create and edit templates, create and manage menus, set your site title, logo and icon, set up your
  homepage. Most come from rewriting existing pages under new titles rather than from scratch.
- **Using WordPress consolidates topic pages, keeps screens.** 25 of 34 screen pages stay as they are.
  Three maintenance pages become one checklist; media guides merge into one; "Where to start" becomes an
  ordered learning path plus one "Key WordPress concepts" page; the FAQ shrinks to the pages people
  actually hit.
- **New pages the fresh-install journey lacks:** write and publish a post, back up your site, keep your
  site secure, troubleshoot common problems, media shortcodes (classic), and a classic default themes index.
- **Classic WordPress shrinks from 36 to 30** and becomes a coherent appendix: one "Using the Classic
  Editor" guide absorbs the excerpt, visibility and sticky pages; theme changelogs all stay because every
  bundled theme's readme links to them.

## Cross-cutting policies (recommendations)

- **Changelog sections:** drop the hand-written "Changelog" H2 from article bodies (about 12,300 words in
  169 articles), archive them once to `data/changelog-archive.json`, and record review dates in a
  `last_reviewed` frontmatter field. Git history and PRs take over the role. Theme changelog pages are
  separate pages and stay.
- **Paired pages:** block-first pages drop the "(Block Editor)" suffix but keep their slugs; only classic
  pages carry a qualifier. Each pair cross-links with one standard note.
- **Version and history pages:** the user docs describe current WordPress and link to history. The 935
  release pages stay where they are because the About screen links them; the release archive tables should
  be generated from data rather than hand-maintained.

Details for each policy are in the Classic WordPress area below.

What the numbers mean:

- **Screens are fine; topic pages are not.** Of the 34 Administration Screens pages, 25 stay as they are: they are reference pages that match wp-admin, most were touched in a June 2024 sweep, and they are what the screen help tabs link to. The problems are in the topic sections. Maintenance & security has three pages giving the same checklist. Media has four pages that point at one another. Formatting has three thin Codex-era pages. Where to start was written for the Codex.
- **Freshness.** 27 of the 97 pages were last changed before 2024. Every one of them is marked REWRITE, MERGE, DELETE or MOVE here; none is kept as is. Separately, nearly every page in scope has 2018–2019 screenshots, KEEP pages included. Refreshing screenshots is a job for the screenshot-automation pass and is not counted as a REWRITE here.
- **Block-first leftovers.** Six pages in scope still describe classic UI as if it were current. `pages-add-new-screen` covers meta boxes and the Visual/Text tabs. `use-images` describes the Add Media button. `the-wordpress-gallery-block-editor` uses the media-modal gallery flow. `content-visibility-block-editor` and `what-is-an-excerpt-block-editor` refer to the removed Document tab. `administration-screens` still tours Widgets, Menus and Customizer. All six are rewritten, merged or deleted. The "(Block Editor)" suffix comes off every title that survives, because block is now the default and Classic WordPress is the exception.
- **Developer material.** Five pages move whole to developer.wordpress.org. Several more keep their user half and lose a code-heavy section on rewrite: `protect-posts-with-password` (customizing the password form and cookie), `post-status` (custom statuses), `revisions` (constants), `what-is-post-type` (queries and templates), `use-image-and-file-attachments` (theme usage), `settings-privacy-screen` (the "To Theme Developers" section) and `manage-plugins` (Developing Plugins).

**Template trim (applies to every block reference page that survives).** Delete the hand-written "Go back to the list of Blocks" line (the hierarchy does that), the generic toolbar walkthrough (mover, drag handle, Transform to, More options), the "Block settings panel … click the cog" paragraph, the Advanced settings list and every "Read about these and other settings" note. What stays on each block page: what the block does, how it differs from neighbours, its block-specific toolbar buttons and settings, and gotchas. This is mechanical and scriptable against the boilerplate sentences already measured (§2 of `notes/helphub-structure-analysis.md`), so it is not counted as REWRITE.

**Block reference model.** One rule, applied by family:

1. **Content blocks** (blocks people insert directly into posts and pages: Text, Media, Design, Widgets) keep **one page per block**. They are what people search for by name ("table block", "cover block"), each has a distinct workflow, and after the trim they are 300–1,500 words of unique material.
2. **Variations live on their parent block's page.** Row, Stack and Grid → Group; Archive Title and Search Results Title → Query Title; Next/Previous Post → Post Navigation Link; Modified Date → Date; Categories and Tags (Post Terms variations) together; the 31 provider embeds → Embed.
3. **Inner blocks live on their container's page.** The 11 comment inner blocks → Comments block; Post Template → Query Loop; Submenu, Custom Link and Home Link → Navigation.
4. **Theme blocks that only make sense in a template are grouped by what they show**: Post blocks, Post meta blocks, Site identity blocks, Archive title blocks. Theme blocks that are substantive on their own keep a page: Query Loop, Navigation, Template Part, Pagination, Breadcrumbs, Terms Query, Login/out.
5. **Deprecated, renamed or removed blocks get no page**: a redirect plus a row in the "Deprecated blocks" table on the Blocks list.

Result: Text 13, Media 9, Design 9, Widgets 12, Theme 13 (from 51), Embeds 1 (from 36), plus the Blocks list index: **58 pages instead of 135**. ### Decisions for humans

1. **Theme blocks as families (51 → 13 pages) vs one page per block.** Proposed: group by container or purpose, keep standalone only blocks that are substantive alone. Alternative: keep one page per block and rely on the template trim alone; that leaves about 40 pages of 150–300 unique words each. Middle option: families for Comments and Site identity only (the most templated), everything else per block.
2. **Featured Image and Content inside "Post blocks" vs standalone.** Post Featured Image is searched for by name and has 834 words. Alternative: keep `post-featured-image-block` (and possibly `post-content-block`) as their own pages and make "Post blocks" a lighter family page. Redirects to anchors keep URLs alive either way.
3. **One Embeds page vs a handful of provider pages.** Proposed: one page with a provider table and per-provider notes. Alternative: keep pages for the 3–4 providers with real per-service steps or search demand (YouTube, Vimeo, Spotify, X) and table the rest. The provider list itself needs checking against the embed variations core registers today; several services (Cloudup, Screencast, Crowdsignal, Amazon Kindle) may have changed or closed.
4. **Row, Stack and Grid merged into Group.** They show as separate blocks in the inserter, so people look for them by name. Alternative: keep three short pages that each link to Group for the shared container settings.
5. **Content blocks stay one page per block.** Consistent rule, but some widget pages are thin after the trim (Archives 58% boilerplate, Calendar, Tag Cloud). Alternative: a "Site archive blocks" family (Archives, Calendar, Categories List, Tag Cloud, Latest Posts, Latest Comments).
6. **Default themes: keep four per-theme guides vs one Default themes page.** Proposed: keep them; they are real user guides to templates, style variations and patterns, and the changelog nesting already depends on them. Alternative: keep Twenty Twenty-Five only and fold 2022–2024 into the section intro (a table linking to the theme directory), which also simplifies the changelog decision. If a newer default theme (Twenty Twenty-Six) has shipped, a page for it is a gap this proposal does not fill; confirm.
7. **Styles split into three.** Proposed: reference + colour/font task + CSS task. Alternative: keep one long Styles page with a task-shaped table of contents. The split is what produces the "Change colors and fonts with Styles" task page without writing it from scratch.
8. **Pattern submission guide moved off HelpHub.** It describes a wordpress.org service for pattern authors, not site editing. Alternative: merge it into Block Pattern Directory as a "Submit your own" section.
9. **Create a Favicon merged across sections into Site Editor > Identity.** Alternative: keep it under Themes (it is a long-standing, well-searched URL) and link from Identity. The redirect preserves the URL in either case.
10. **Retitles that change familiar names.** "Template Editor" → "Create and edit templates", "Synced Patterns (Reusable blocks)" → "Synced patterns", "Work with themes" → "Find, install and activate a theme". Slugs stay; the question is whether search users still need the old term in the title. Proposed: old term goes in the first paragraph, not the title.

### Policies

#### a. Changelog sections

**Numbers** (from `raw/articles.json`, text after the last `Changelog` H2): 169 articles per
`article-facts.json` (171 with a looser heading match), **≈12,300 words**, about 3.9% of all article text
(317,676 words). They hold ≈870 dated "Created/Updated YYYY-MM-DD" entries and ≈380 `@user` props. The median
section is 48 words; the largest is `blocks-list` (886). 141 of the articles have a 2026 entry, so the habit is
alive. The 15 theme `*-changelog` *articles* (26,353 words) are not covered by this policy: they are product
release notes linked from `readme.txt` and stay as pages.

**Recommendation: drop from the body, archive once, add `last_reviewed`.**

1. One-time export of every section to `data/changelog-archive.json`, keyed by slug: `{date, props[], notes[]}`
   per entry, plus the raw HTML for fidelity. Not published as a page; it is a contributor and credit record.
2. Remove the H2 and its list from the Markdown in the same conversion pass (the converter already knows the
   `div.changelog` group).
3. Add frontmatter `last_reviewed: YYYY-MM-DD` (newest entry) and, optionally, `reviewed_for: "7.1"`. From
   then on, a PR that reviews a page bumps `last_reviewed`; git history and the PR record what changed and who.
4. Props move to the PR: a co-author trailer or the props bot convention used in the Gutenberg repo.

Caveat: the importer strips frontmatter, so `last_reviewed` reaches wordpress.org only if the HelpHub importer
subclass maps it to post meta. Without that, the live page still shows "Last updated" from `post_modified`.

**Alternative: keep the sections in the body.** Familiar to the docs team, visible to readers, needs no tooling.
Cost: duplicates git, produces merge conflicts at the end of every file when two PRs touch one page, and keeps
12k words of reader-irrelevant text in search excerpts and translations (Rosetta locales translate them too).

Classic-only suffixed titles with no twin: `write-posts-classic-editor`, `sticky-posts-classic-editor`,
`write-code-in-your-posts-classic-editor`, `appearance-widgets-screen-classic-editor`. One unsuffixed pair:
`block-based-widgets-editor` ↔ `appearance-widgets-screen-classic-editor`.

**Recommendation.**

- Block-first titles drop the suffix: "First steps with WordPress", "Inserting images into posts and pages",
  "The WordPress Gallery", "Set content visibility", "What is an excerpt?", "Use keyboard shortcuts".
  The block editor is the default, so it needs no qualifier.
- **Slugs do not change.** URLs are flat and stable (see `notes/helphub-structure-analysis.md` §4); renaming
  six slugs buys nothing a reader sees and costs six redirects plus Rosetta link breakage.
- Only Classic WordPress pages carry a qualifier, and it names what the reader has installed: "(Classic Editor)"
  for editor pages, "Classic Widgets" for the widgets plugin. Keep it even inside the section, because search
  results and wp-admin help links show the title without its section.
- Cross-reference with one standard line, not ad-hoc notes. Block page, first line after the H1:
  `> [!NOTE]` "Using the Classic Editor plugin? See [Using the Classic Editor › Excerpt](…#excerpt)." Classic page:
  the mirror line back to the block page. Today each pair words this differently, and some pairs lack it.
- Alternative: a frontmatter field `classic_counterpart: <slug>#anchor` rendered as the note. Cleaner, but it needs the
  importer subclass and a theme change; the hand-written line works today and can be generated from the field later.

#### c. Version and history pages

The actions for these pages belong to the overview reviewer; this is the policy they should apply.

**Principle: the user docs describe current WordPress and link to history; they do not restate release notes.**

- **Release pages (935, `helphub_version`).** Out of scope for the migration and they stay where they are, on the
  `helphub_version` post type at `/documentation/wordpress-version/…`. `wp-admin/about.php` builds that URL for
  every release, and the theme changelogs link it too, so it is a live contract. The docs link to them
  absolutely (the converter already does). Moving them into Git, if ever, is a separate data project: one
  file per major or structured frontmatter, generated, not 935 hand-edited pages in the docs tree.
- **`wordpress-versions` (Release archive).** Keep: it is the landing page `/wordpress-version/` redirects to
  and the one index of releases. Treat its tables as data: generate them from `raw/versions.json` plus the
  major-release terms, rather than hand-adding a row each release. `wordpress-versions-0-7-to-4-9` exists only to
  keep one page short; once generated, fold it into the archive with collapsed majors (redirect) or keep it as a
  generated second page. Either is fine; generated is the point.
- **`supported-versions`.** Keep. 192 words of support policy is what readers need; link to the core security
  FAQ for detail.
- **`learn-about-wordpress-and-version-history`.** Keep the origins narrative (b2/cafelog, licence, community).
  Most of its 3,590 words are one paragraph per major release, 0.7 to 7.1, with contributor counts and
  highlights, edited every release. Cut that to a year-by-year line linking the News post and the Release archive.

**Alternative:** treat the docs as the canonical history and curate per-version summaries in the user docs.
Rejected: it duplicates News posts and release pages, and it ages faster than anything else in the tree.

### Decisions for humans

1. **Theme stubs.** Twenty Fourteen and Twenty Thirteen have generated sections whose only child is a changelog.
   Proposal: drop the two stubs and hang their changelogs directly under the new index. That is a small
   `structure.yaml` change; the alternative is writing two theme guides, which the frozen-appendix rule argues against.
2. **Merge targets keep old slugs** (`write-posts-classic-editor` for "Using the Classic Editor",
   `inserting-images-into-posts-and-pages-classic` for "Add media…"). Slug and title then disagree. Accept, or
   rename with redirects? Core links `write-posts-classic-editor`, so a rename needs a core patch or a permanent redirect.
3. **`media-shortcodes`.** Keep shortcodes in the user docs (users type them in the Shortcode block), or move the
   reference to developer.wordpress.org? This proposal keeps them.
4. **Changelog policy (a)** is a docs-team process change, not just a migration choice. It needs sign-off from the
   team that writes them, and a decision on how props are credited in PRs.
5. **`last_reviewed` on wordpress.org** requires the HelpHub importer subclass to map it to meta and the theme to
   show it. Worth it, or rely on "Last updated"?
6. **Block-theme default theme changelogs** (4, outside this scope) follow the same KEEP rule; the overview/editing
   reviewers should not merge them away.
